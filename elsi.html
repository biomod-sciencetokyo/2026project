In considering the ethical, legal, and social issues (ELSI) [1] of our project, we referred to the Ethical Principles of Molecular Robotics, which were formulated by the Japanese molecular robotics community through dialogue with social scientists and refined with input from young researchers, including BIOMOD participants [2, 3]. The principles consist of four items: comprehensive assessment of risks and benefits, consideration for safety and environment, attention to security and dual-use issues, and ensuring accountability and transparency. The issues below are discussed with these principles in mind.

1. Ethical Issues

Verification of Off-Target Disruption Risks and Safety

Because this technology disrupts target membranes by exploiting the structural rigidity of DNA, the possibility that it acts on membranes other than the intended targets—extracellular vesicles (EVs) and cancer cells—must be considered. In particular, unintended disruption of normal cell membranes could damage healthy tissues. Therefore, for therapeutic applications, it is necessary to verify the specificity of target recognition and the selectivity of membrane disruption, and to establish in vivo safety in order to reduce the risk of new side effects.

Implementation of Informed Consent

At the stages of clinical research and future practical application, obtaining informed consent from patients is critical. To respect patients' right to make their own decisions, a robust communication framework must be established to provide clear and honest explanations, ensuring patients fully understand and accept the mechanisms, potential risks, and expected benefits.

Prevention of Dual-Use Misuse

Although this technology is being developed for medical applications, its core function—selectively disrupting lipid membranes in response to a specific nucleic acid sequence—could in principle be redirected toward harmful purposes, such as damaging normal cells. Following the principle of attention to security and dual-use issues, researchers must carefully consider this risk when sharing design information and take appropriate measures to ensure that the technology is used only for legitimate and beneficial purposes.

Environmental Impact and Disposal

In line with the principle of consideration for safety and environment, the potential effects of our artificially designed DNA nanostructures on the environment after use must also be considered. In particular, these structures may enter the environment through biological waste or improper disposal, and their effects on organisms and ecosystems are not yet fully understood. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate their environmental impact and establish appropriate disposal methods to minimize potential ecological risks.

2. Legal Issues

Protection of Intellectual Property and Clarification of AI-Generated Output

Outcomes generated from this research activity (including data, DNA designs, algorithms, and experimental know-how) constitute critical intellectual property. Protecting IP rights is essential not only for pursuing commercial value, but also for preventing unauthorized monopolization or misuse by third parties, thereby ensuring appropriate societal benefits. Furthermore, when leveraging AI for DNA sequence design or computational simulations, proper rights management aligned with relevant laws and guidelines must be ensured to avoid ambiguities surrounding the ownership of copyrights and patent rights for generated outputs [4].

Compliance with Medical Regulations (PMDA Approval)

To apply this technology clinically for therapeutic purposes, it must undergo rigorous evaluation and receive approval from regulatory agencies, such as Japan's Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), under the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act [5].

Adherence to Biosafety Regulations

To guarantee the safety of research processes involving artificial DNA sequence design and nucleic acids, experiments must strictly comply with relevant laws and guidelines applicable to research involving nucleic acids, as well as laboratory safety standards.

Regulatory Gaps for Non-Living DNA Nanostructures

Because our DNA nanostructures are made of synthetic nucleic acids and cannot self-replicate, they do not meet the definition of an "organism" under the Cartagena Act, which covers only cells capable of transferring or replicating nucleic acids, viruses, and viroids, and may therefore fall outside its scope [3, 6]. Existing laws and regulations may therefore not fully address the challenges associated with their application, including safety assessment, clinical application, and appropriate management. It is thus necessary to review and update regulatory frameworks in accordance with technological advances to ensure the safe and responsible development and application of this technology.

3. Social Issues

Addressing Global Healthcare Disparities

Treatments utilizing advanced DNA nanotechnology may involve high development and manufacturing costs, carrying the risk of escalating treatment costs and exacerbating global healthcare disparities due to unevenly distributed manufacturing infrastructure. The feasibility of implementation differs markedly between high-income countries or affluent patient populations and low-income regions with underdeveloped medical infrastructure.

Fostering Public Acceptance and Trust

The public may harbor anxieties or concerns regarding research involving genetic manipulation. Consequently, proactive efforts must be made to clearly explain the underlying mechanisms, control frameworks, and safety measures to foster accurate public understanding and trust.
