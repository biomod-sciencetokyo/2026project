Abstract

  1. 01

    Challenge

    Extracellular vesicles (EVs) in blood and other body fluids carry RNA and proteins that reflect the state of the cells that released them, making them promising sources for disease diagnosis. To read these molecules, the EV membrane must be opened. However, conventional methods such as surfactants open every vesicle at once, so the information on which EV each molecule came from is lost.

    Project →
  2. 02

    Approach

    Our idea is to build the membrane-breaking tool on site, only where it is needed. We developed Bundled HCR, an extension of the Hybridization Chain Reaction: starting from a seed, six kinds of double-hairpin DNAs open one after another, so that many DNA strands grow together into a thick, rigid bundle instead of a single thin strand. Cholesterol tags anchor the growing bundle to the membrane, and its stiffness bends the membrane until it breaks. Because the reaction starts only where a trigger is present, and in our design the trigger sits on the surface of the target EV, only that vesicle is disrupted.

    Project →
  3. 03

    Simulation

    We used 2D coarse-grained molecular dynamics and a 3D free-energy model to find out what kind of DNA bundle can open a pore in a membrane, varying its rigidity, the length of the linker that connects it to the cholesterol anchor, and the vesicle size. We also checked whether our designed HCR reaction can proceed.

    Results Stiffer bundles were the ones that started a pore in the model (defined as the membrane being stretched beyond a set threshold). Short linkers worked better for small vesicles (R = 50 nm), and long linkers for large ones (R = 200 nm). The 3D model pointed to about 12 helices per bundle as the design target. For the reaction, every designed HCR step releases free energy (ΔG < 0), so the cascade can proceed in principle.

    Simulation →
  4. 04

    Experiment

    We ran two experiments, each focusing on one part of our system. The first tested whether Bundled HCR actually proceeds, using a minimal design that grows into a 6-helix DNA bundle. The second tested whether rigid, cholesterol-modified 12- and 13-helix bundles, the size suggested by our simulations, anchor to lipid membranes.

    Results Gel electrophoresis and AFM showed long bundles growing from the seed by Bundled HCR. Fluorescence microscopy showed that cholesterol-modified 12-helix bundles anchor to giant vesicles (GUVs). Whether the bundles break the membrane has not been tested yet; we will test it with a calcein leakage assay.

    Experiment →
  5. 05

    Discussion

    Conclusion Simulations identified DNA Bundle rigidity, linker length, and vesicle size as key design parameters for membrane disruption, and the designed HCR cascade is thermodynamically favorable. Experiments showed that Bundled HCR elongates DNA Bundles and that cholesterol-modified bundles anchor to lipid membranes; demonstrating membrane disruption is the next step.

    Future Potentially, our research may pave the way for molecular signal-responsive systems capable of lipid membrane disruption. By using cell-free cancer-associated miRNAs as sequence-specific triggers, such systems could eventually be extended from lipid vesicles toward larger membrane-bound targets, including living cells.
