Abstract

  1. 01

    Challenge

    Extracellular vesicles (EVs) in blood and other body fluids carry RNA and proteins that reflect the state of the cells that released them, making them promising sources for disease diagnosis. To read these molecules, the EV membrane must be opened, while keeping track of which EV each molecule came from.

    Existing methods fall short. Surfactants dissolve every vesicle at once, so this origin information is lost. Pre-assembled DNA nanostructures that damage membranes are always active, so they also attack membranes that are not the target.

    Our Goal A physical membrane disruption system that becomes active only near the target EV.

    Project →
  2. 02

    Approach

    We build the membrane-breaking tool on site, only where it is needed, in three steps. Step 1 Trigger: a Trigger DNA near the target EV switches a seed on. Step 2 Elongation: from the seed, Bundled HCR grows a rigid, cholesterol-tagged DNA bundle. Step 3 Membrane disruption: the cholesterol tags anchor the bundle, and its stiffness bends the membrane until it breaks. Because the bundle forms only where the trigger is, only the target EV is disrupted.

    This Project As a proof of concept, we demonstrated Steps 2 and 3.

    Project →
  3. 03

    Key Factor: Bundled HCR

    Conventional HCR, a DNA-only chain reaction, grows a single thin double strand. We extended it into Bundled HCR. A seed made of six strands is kept inactive by a Blocker strand until the Trigger DNA removes it. The active seed then opens six kinds of double-hairpin DNAs (H1–H6) in a fixed order, again and again, so that many double strands grow together into one thick, rigid bundle. Changing the seed and the number of hairpin types sets the number of strands: six in our minimal design, about twelve for membrane disruption.

    Project →
  4. 04

    Simulation

    We used 2D coarse-grained molecular dynamics and a 3D free-energy model to find out what kind of DNA bundle can open a pore in a membrane (Step 3), varying its rigidity, the length of the linker that connects it to the cholesterol anchor, and the vesicle size. We also checked whether our designed HCR reaction can proceed (Step 2).

    Results Stiffer bundles were the ones that started a pore in the model (defined as the membrane being stretched beyond a set threshold). Short linkers worked better for small vesicles (R = 50 nm), and long linkers for large ones (R = 200 nm). The 3D model pointed to about 12 helices per bundle as the design target. For the reaction, every designed HCR step releases free energy (ΔG < 0), so the cascade can proceed in principle.

    Simulation →
  5. 05

    Experiment

    We ran two experiments, each focusing on one step of our system. The first tested whether Bundled HCR actually proceeds (Step 2), using a minimal design that grows into a 6-helix DNA bundle. The second tested whether rigid, cholesterol-modified 12- and 13-helix bundles, the size suggested by our simulations, anchor to lipid membranes (Step 3).

    Results Gel electrophoresis and AFM showed long bundles growing from the seed by Bundled HCR. Fluorescence microscopy showed that cholesterol-modified 12-helix bundles anchor to giant vesicles (GUVs). Whether the bundles break the membrane has not been tested yet.

  6. 06

    Discussion

    Conclusion Bundled HCR builds DNA bundles from a seed, and rigid cholesterol-modified bundles anchor to lipid membranes. Our simulations give design guides for breaking the membrane, but membrane disruption itself has not been demonstrated yet.

    Future Works We will test membrane disruption with a calcein leakage assay and on EV-sized vesicles, and demonstrate Step 1: a seed that switches on only when the Trigger DNA, attached to an antibody or aptamer that binds the target EV, removes its Blocker strand. Combining all three steps would achieve our goal. Further ahead, cancer-associated miRNAs could serve as triggers, extending the system toward living cells.
