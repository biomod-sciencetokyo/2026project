1. Conclusion

Our goal: when a trigger arrives, rigid DNA Bundles grow right on the lipid membrane (Bundled HCR) and break it open by mechanical force.

What simulations told us

  • ✓ Stiffer Bundles were commonly linked to local pore formation (2D coarse-grained model).
  • ✓ Linker length depends on vesicle size: short for small vesicles (R = 50 nm), long for large ones (R = 200 nm).
  • ✓ Helix count per Bundle: a model-based design guide from the 3D free-energy model and helix-count screening.
  • ✓ HCR runs on its own (NUPACK): hairpins stay >99.7% monomeric, and every step has a negative ΔG.

What experiments showed

  • ✓ Seed assembles from S1–S6 (PAGE).
  • ✓ Bundles elongate by Bundled HCR: high-molecular-weight bands (agarose gel) and long structures (AFM).
  • ✓ 12- and 13-helix DNA bundles form (PAGE, AFM).
  • ✓ Cholesterol anchors bundles to GUVs; 200 mM NaCl suppresses nonspecific binding (fluorescence microscopy).
  • ✗ Membrane disruption: not confirmed yet. It will be evaluated by a calcein leakage assay in future experiments.

Bottom line: the design basis for an HCR-programmed, membrane-disrupting DNA machine is in place. Next up: proving the disruption itself.

2. Limitations

Our goal is to develop a physical membrane disruption system that becomes active only near target EVs, enabling us to access the molecules inside specific EVs without losing their origin information. Compared to this goal, our current prototype has three main limitations:

01

Lack of Target-Specific Activation

The current HCR seed has no activation control mechanism, and the cholesterol anchors can bind non-specifically to any lipid membrane. Without target-specific regulation, DNA bundles may form in bulk solution or attach to non-target vesicles.

02

Unverified Membrane Rupture on EV-Sized Vesicles

So far, we have only confirmed bundle elongation and membrane anchoring on GUVs. We have not yet experimentally demonstrated actual membrane rupture (such as via a calcein leakage assay) or tested our system on small vesicles comparable in size to EVs (40–160 nm in diameter).

03

Access to Released Internal Molecules

Even if target EVs are selectively disrupted, the released internal molecules (such as RNA and proteins) will diffuse into the surrounding solution. A method to collect or analyze these molecules while preserving information about which EV they came from is still needed.

3. Future

3.1 Improved Future Design

To overcome each of the limitations above, we propose three improvements:

Selective Seed Activation via Blocking Strands and Target Recognition

Approach: Combine molecular recognition on the target EV surface with toehold-mediated strand displacement so that the seed turns on only near the target EV.

Mechanism: A complementary blocking strand is pre-hybridized to the initiation site of the seed to keep it in an inactive (“OFF”) state [1]. The Trigger DNA is attached to an antibody or designed as a DNA aptamer that binds to a specific surface antigen on the target EV [2, 3]. By designing a short toehold domain on the blocking strand, unwanted strand displacement is suppressed in dilute bulk solution; once the Trigger binds and accumulates on the target EV surface, the increased local proximity drives toehold-mediated strand displacement, switching the seed “ON” only on the target membrane [4].

Optimization for EV-Sized Membrane Rupture

Approach: Apply the mechanical parameters identified in our simulations to achieve and verify physical membrane rupture on small vesicles.

Mechanism: Based on our 3D model (Simulation, Section 1.2), we will expand the hairpin design to 12 helices to obtain sufficient bending rigidity (BDNA). Guided by our 2D simulations (Simulation, Section 1.1, Figs. 2 and 4), we will also use a short linker (l0 ≈ 2.5 nm) to effectively transmit mechanical stress to small, highly curved vesicles (R ≈ 50 nm). We will then evaluate membrane rupture on nanopore-extruded small vesicles using a calcein leakage assay.

Compartmentalization for Recovering Internal Molecules

Approach: Combine selective disruption with micro-compartmentalization so that target EVs can be lysed without interference from co-existing non-target EVs.

Mechanism: In biological fluids, non-target EVs vastly outnumber target EVs, meaning multiple vesicles are often encapsulated together within a single microfluidic droplet or microwell [5]. Unlike surfactants that would lyse all co-encapsulated vesicles indiscriminately, our system selectively disrupts only the target EVs inside each compartment, allowing their internal molecules to be recovered and analyzed while keeping their origin information intact.

3.2 Applications

Selective Physical Disruption of Cancer Cells

Conventional chemotherapies and molecularly targeted drugs often face acquired drug resistance caused by drug-efflux pumps, target mutations, and altered intracellular signaling pathways [6]. Therefore, physical approaches that directly disrupt cancer cell membranes using mechanical force or membrane-perturbing nanostructures are being explored as a way to overcome biochemical resistance [7, 8]. By attaching the Trigger DNA to antibodies or DNA aptamers that recognize cancer-specific cell-surface antigens [9], our blocked HCR seed can be activated only upon contact with cancer cells. The resulting rigid DNA bundles mechanically rupture the plasma membrane, offering a physical therapeutic approach independent of intracellular chemical pathways.

Stimuli-Responsive Drug Delivery Systems (DDS)

Conventional liposomal drug carriers often suffer from premature drug leakage in the bloodstream and poor control over release at disease sites, leading to systemic side effects [10]. To achieve controlled release, we propose anchoring blocked HCR seeds onto drug-loaded liposomes. The carriers remain stable until encountering disease-specific extracellular biomarkers at the target site, where Trigger binding initiates in situ DNA bundle assembly and physically ruptures the liposomal membrane to release the drug locally. For future in vivo bloodstream applications, protecting surface-exposed DNA seeds and triggers against nuclease degradation—such as through chemical backbone modifications or PEG shielding—will also be an important consideration [11].

Programmable Membrane Remodeling in Artificial Cells

Physical membrane disruption and non-destructive membrane deformation lie on a continuous mechanical spectrum: below the critical stress threshold required to open a pore, localized mechanical loading bends and reshapes the lipid bilayer without breaking it [12]. Using this principle, our system can be applied as a stimuli-responsive “artificial cytoskeleton” in bottom-up synthetic biology [13]. Our 3D reduced free-energy simulations show that changing the DNA bundle helix count (bending rigidity) and cholesterol-mediated adhesion energy (α) alters local membrane curvature (Simulation, Section 1.2, Figs. 12–14). Operating below the rupture threshold will enable chemically triggered bending, budding, and shape changes in giant liposomes.
