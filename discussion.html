1. Conclusion

Our goal was a trigger-responsive system that assembles rigid DNA Bundles directly on a lipid membrane by Bundled HCR and disrupts the membrane by mechanical force.

Simulations identified the design parameters that matter. In the 2D coarse-grained screen, high DNA Bundle bending rigidity was commonly associated with the model-defined local pore-formation event, and the favorable linker length depended on vesicle size: shorter linkers for small vesicles (R = 50 nm) and longer linkers for large vesicles (R = 200 nm). The 3D reduced free-energy model and the helix-count screening provided a model-dependent design guide for the number of helices per Bundle. Thermodynamic analysis with NUPACK showed that the designed hairpins remain more than 99.7% monomeric and that every sequential HCR step has a negative free-energy change, supporting a spontaneous cascade.

Experimentally, PAGE confirmed that the seed assembles from S1–S6, and PAGE and AFM indicated that the 12- and 13-helix DNA bundles form as elongated structures. [Results on Bundled HCR elongation, membrane anchoring, and membrane disruption to be added.]

Together, these results lay the design basis for a membrane-disrupting DNA machine whose assembly is programmed by HCR. The remaining limitations and the next steps are discussed below.

2. Limitations

In the current prototype, the HCR initiation complex (“seed”) does not incorporate a molecular gate mechanism, and there is concern that the cholesterol anchor may bind to off-target lipid membranes. Without precise spatial control, DNA bundles may form unintentionally in solution or anchor to off-target vesicles. To overcome this challenge, a reliable molecular switch is needed that initiates HCR only on the surface of the target membrane.

3. Future

3.1 Improved Future Design

To prevent unintended background reactions and binding to off-target membranes, we are conceptualizing a molecular switch that integrates on-off control of the “seed” with recognition of the target membrane surface. First, by pre-conjugating a complementary blocking strand to the reaction initiation site of the “seed,” we keep the HCR reaction completely “OFF” in the initial state. Furthermore, to activate this switch only on the membrane of specific extracellular vesicles (EVs), we either modify the trigger strand at the terminal end of an antibody or design it as a DNA aptamer that selectively binds to the surface antigen of the target EV. When the trigger bound to the membrane of the target EV binds to the tow-hold region of the blocking strand, the blocking strand is released via a tow-hold strand displacement reaction, and the “seed” switches to the “ON” state. As a result, DNA bundles form locally only on the membrane of the target EV, physically disrupting the membrane. This makes it possible to recover the contents while retaining biomarker colocalization information at the single-particle level.

3.2 Applications

Selective, Physical Destruction of Cancer Cells

Cancer remains a global health crisis. Conventional molecularly targeted drugs face challenges such as difficulty in escaping endosomes, delivery limitations, and the development of drug resistance. We envision applying this system as a physical, non-chemical molecular therapy. By programming the HCR initiation complex to recognize cancer-specific biomarkers such as miR-21, the reaction is triggered only upon contact with malignant cells. The rigid DNA bundles formed then pierce and destroy the cancer cell membrane through pure mechanical force, thereby circumventing conventional chemical drug resistance pathways.

Stimulus-Responsive Drug Delivery Systems (DDS)

Conventional liposome-based drug carriers face challenges that lead to systemic side effects due to premature drug leakage into the bloodstream and difficulties in controlling drug release at the target site. To maximize therapeutic efficacy, a mechanism that releases the drug in strict response to signals specific to the lesion site is essential. We envision developing the HCR-driven membrane disruption mechanism of this system into a smart, stimulus-responsive release switch for DDS. By incorporating masked HCR “seeds” onto the surface of drug-encapsulated liposomes, the carrier remains stable during circulation in the bloodstream. Upon encountering disease-specific biomarkers in the target lesion microenvironment, the trigger initiates the formation of DNA bundles on the liposome surface, physically disrupting the lipid bilayer to enable on-demand, localized drug release.

Programmable Control of Artificial Cell Deformation

In bottom-up synthetic biology and molecular robotics, reproducing dynamic cellular behaviors such as division, budding, and morphological adaptation remains a major challenge. Unlike rigid, static structures, living cells dynamically remodel their membrane morphology in response to external signals. We envision utilizing this system as a programmable, stimulus-responsive “artificial cytoskeleton.” Simulations in this study suggest that local membrane curvature changes when the bending stiffness or cross-linking efficiency of DNA bundles is varied, indicating the potential to induce membrane deformation within a range below the membrane rupture threshold. In the future, by optimizing these structural parameters, we expect to be able to induce morphological changes—such as bending and budding of giant liposomes—in response to chemical stimuli, thereby paving the way for programmable shape remodeling in artificial cells.
