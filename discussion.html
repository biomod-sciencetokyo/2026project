1. Conclusion

Our goal: when a trigger arrives, rigid DNA Bundles grow directly on the lipid membrane (Bundled HCR) and rupture it mechanically.

What simulations told us

  • ✓ Higher DNA Bundle bending rigidity favored local pore formation in the 2D coarse-grained model (Simulation 1.1).
  • ✓ The favorable linker length depended on vesicle size in the same model: short linkers for small vesicles (R = 50 nm) and long linkers for larger vesicles (R = 200 nm) (Simulation 1.1).
  • ✓ About 12 helices per DNA Bundle are required: in the 3D reduced free-energy model, 12 helices reached the target curvature even at the lowest crosslinking efficiency examined. The 13-Helix DNA Bundle was prepared as a one-helix-above-threshold comparison design (Simulation 1.2).
  • ✓ The designed Bundled HCR sequences are thermodynamically feasible: in NUPACK equilibrium analysis, each hairpin was predicted to remain >99.7% monomeric, and every intended hybridization step had a negative ΔG. This analysis does not address reaction kinetics (Simulation 2).

What experiments showed

  • ✓ The seed assembled from S1–S6 (PAGE; Experiment 1.1).
  • ✓ The Bundled HCR mechanism produced elongated structures as designed: high-molecular-weight products appeared on agarose gels, and elongated structures were observed by AFM (Experiment 1.2, 1.3). However, a smear was also observed in the H1–H6-only lane, suggesting some seed-independent leakage.
  • ✓ 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles were assembled by annealing (gel electrophoresis, AFM; Experiment 2.1, 2.2).
  • ✓ Cholesterol-modified 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles bound to GUVs. In the presence of 200 mM NaCl, only the cholesterol-modified DNA Bundles remained bound, indicating that cholesterol-dependent anchoring was observed under these conditions (fluorescence microscopy; Experiment 2.3).

Bottom line: we demonstrated the two core steps separately: elongation of a DNA Bundle by Bundled HCR, and cholesterol-dependent membrane anchoring of rigid 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles. Our simulations provided design guidelines for helix count and linker length.

Next step: membrane disruption itself has not yet been tested. We will evaluate it with a calcein leakage assay (Experiment 3.1) on vesicles comparable in size to EVs (Experiment 3.2), the size range for which our simulations predict rupture conditions.

2. Limitations

Our goal in this project was to grow a rigid DNA Bundle by Bundled HCR and disrupt the lipid membrane with it (Project 3.2). Measured against this goal, and toward our longer-term aim of selectively disrupting target EVs, our current system has two main limitations:

01

Lack of Target-Specific Activation

The current Bundled HCR seed has no activation control mechanism, and cholesterol inserts into any lipid membrane. Our data already point to two sources of off-target activity. First, the smear in the H1–H6-only lane of the agarose gel (Experiment 1.2) suggests that hairpins can polymerize to some extent without the seed (leakage), so DNA Bundles may form in bulk solution. Second, in the standard buffer containing 12 mM MgCl2, even unmodified DNA Bundles bound to DOPC GUVs (Experiment 2.3), showing that membrane binding depends on buffer conditions and lipid composition, not only on cholesterol. Without target-specific regulation, DNA Bundles could therefore also attach to non-target vesicles.

02

Membrane Rupture Not Yet Tested on EV-Sized Vesicles

Our simulations predict rupture conditions for vesicles with radii of 50–300 nm, i.e., in the size range of EVs (40–160 nm in diameter) (Simulation 1.1). All of our experiments, however, used GUVs, which are micrometers in size and therefore far outside the simulated range; at the length scale of a DNA Bundle, a GUV membrane is almost flat. Even a rupture assay on GUVs would thus not directly test the model's predictions, and the absence of rupture on GUVs would not contradict them.

The experiments we performed therefore confirm DNA Bundle elongation and membrane anchoring, whereas membrane rupture has not yet been tested experimentally. Testing the model requires a rupture assay (e.g., a calcein leakage assay) on small vesicles comparable in size to EVs.

3. Future

3.1 Improved Future Design

To overcome each of the limitations above, we propose two improvements:

01

Selective Seed Activation via Blocking Strands and Target Recognition

Approach: Combine molecular recognition on the target EV surface with toehold-mediated strand displacement so that the seed turns on only near the target EV.

Mechanism: A complementary blocking strand is pre-hybridized to the initiation site of the seed to keep it in an inactive (“OFF”) state [1]. The Trigger DNA is attached to an antibody, or designed as part of a DNA aptamer, that binds to a surface protein of the target EV, such as a tetraspanin or a disease-associated antigen [2, 3]. The blocking strand carries only a short toehold, so strand displacement is too slow to proceed at the low Trigger DNA concentration in bulk solution.

Why activation is local: the rate of toehold-mediated strand displacement scales with the concentration of the invading strand. When many Trigger DNAs bind to antigens on the same EV, they are confined to a region only tens of nanometers across, together with the membrane-anchored seeds on that EV. The effective local concentration of Trigger DNA around these seeds is therefore orders of magnitude higher than in bulk, so displacement of the blocking strand, and thus Bundled HCR, starts only on the target membrane [4]. Specificity could be raised further by splitting the trigger into two halves carried by two different recognition elements, so that the seed is activated only on EVs that present both antigens [4].

02

Optimization for EV-Sized Membrane Rupture

Approach: Apply the mechanical parameters identified in our simulations to achieve and verify physical membrane rupture on small vesicles.

Rationale: Our simulations predict rupture conditions only for EV-sized vesicles, whereas our experiments used GUVs, which lie far outside this range. Separating what the model predicts from what we tested experimentally, the next essential experiment is to test rupture on vesicles in the simulated size range.

Mechanism: Based on our 3D model (Simulation 1.2), we will expand the hairpin design to 12 helices to obtain sufficient bending rigidity (BDNA). Guided by our 2D simulations (Simulation 1.1, Figures 2 and 4), we will also use a short linker (l0 ≈ 2.5 nm) to effectively transmit mechanical stress to small, highly curved vesicles (R ≈ 50 nm). We will then evaluate membrane rupture on nanopore-extruded small vesicles using a calcein leakage assay.

3.2 Applications

Selective Physical Disruption of Cancer Cells

Conventional chemotherapies and molecularly targeted drugs often face acquired drug resistance caused by drug-efflux pumps, target mutations, and altered intracellular signaling pathways [5]. Therefore, physical approaches that directly disrupt cancer cell membranes using mechanical force or membrane-perturbing nanostructures are being explored as a way to overcome biochemical resistance [6, 7]. By attaching the Trigger DNA to antibodies or DNA aptamers that recognize cancer-specific cell-surface antigens [8], our blocked Bundled HCR seed could be activated only upon contact with cancer cells. The resulting rigid DNA Bundles would mechanically rupture the plasma membrane, offering a physical therapeutic approach independent of intracellular chemical pathways.

Stimuli-Responsive Drug Delivery Systems (DDS)

Conventional liposomal drug carriers often suffer from premature drug leakage in the bloodstream and poor control over release at disease sites, leading to systemic side effects [9]. To achieve controlled release, we propose anchoring blocked Bundled HCR seeds onto drug-loaded liposomes. The carriers would remain stable until they encounter disease-specific extracellular biomarkers at the target site, where Trigger DNA binding initiates in situ DNA Bundle assembly and physically ruptures the liposomal membrane to release the drug locally. For future in vivo bloodstream applications, protecting surface-exposed seeds and Trigger DNAs against nuclease degradation—such as through chemical backbone modifications or PEG shielding—will also be an important consideration [10].

Programmable Membrane Remodeling in Artificial Cells

Physical membrane disruption and non-destructive membrane deformation lie on a continuous mechanical spectrum: below the critical stress threshold required to open a pore, localized mechanical loading bends and reshapes the lipid bilayer without breaking it [11]. On the basis of this principle, our system could be applied as a stimuli-responsive “artificial cytoskeleton” in bottom-up synthetic biology [12]. Our 3D reduced free-energy model indicates that changing the DNA Bundle helix count (bending rigidity) and the crosslinking efficiency between helices (α) alters local membrane curvature (Simulation 1.2, Figures 13 and 14). Operating below the rupture threshold could enable chemically triggered bending, budding, and shape changes in giant liposomes.
