1. Conclusion

Our goal: grow a rigid DNA Bundle by Bundled HCR and disrupt the lipid membrane with it (Steps 2 and 3; Project 3.2).

What simulations told us

  • ◐ Higher DNA Bundle bending rigidity favored local pore formation (2D model; Simulation 1.1).
  • ◐ The preferred linker length depended on vesicle size: short for R = 50 nm, long for R = 200 nm (Simulation 1.1).
  • ◐ About 12 helices are estimated to be sufficient, even at the lowest crosslinking efficiency examined (3D model; Simulation 1.2).
  • ◐ The designed Bundled HCR steps are thermodynamically favorable: hairpins >99.7% monomeric, all ΔG < 0 (NUPACK, equilibrium only; Simulation 2).

What experiments showed

  • ◐ Bundled HCR gave products consistent with elongation (gel, AFM; Experiments 1.1–1.3), but the H1–H6-only lane also showed a smear, so seed dependence is not yet established.
  • ✓ Annealed, cholesterol-modified 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles (Experiments 2.1, 2.2) bound to GUVs. With 200 mM NaCl, only the cholesterol-modified Bundles stayed bound, and the 12- and 13-Helix Bundles did not differ (Experiment 2.3).

✓ observed experimentally  ·  ◐ model prediction, or only partially supported by experiment

Bottom line: Step 2 is consistent with elongation, but seed dependence is not yet quantified. For Step 3, we showed its prerequisite, cholesterol-dependent anchoring of annealed 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles. Membrane disruption has not been tested yet: we plan a calcein leakage assay on GUVs (Experiment 3.1) and then on EV-sized vesicles (Experiment 3.2).

2. Limitations

Against this goal, and toward our longer-term aim of selectively disrupting target EVs, our current system has four limitations:

01

Seed-Dependent Elongation and Leakage Not Yet Quantified

The H1–H6-only lane also showed a smear (Experiment 1.2), suggesting seed-independent leakage. Our gel and AFM data are qualitative, and NUPACK is an equilibrium calculation that does not predict rates (Simulation 2.2), so elongation and leakage cannot yet be separated.

02

Elongation and Membrane Anchoring Not Yet Combined

Bundled HCR was shown only with a cholesterol-free 6-Helix DNA Bundle (Experiment A), whereas the cholesterol-modified 12- and 13-Helix DNA Bundles used for anchoring were annealed (Experiment B). A cholesterol-modified Bundle grown by Bundled HCR on a membrane (Project 3) remains to be shown.

03

Membrane Rupture and Model Predictions Not Yet Tested

The model predictions are unvalidated and simplified: the 2D model uses a pore-formation proxy (Simulation 1.1), and the 3D model is static with a phenomenological helix-count screening (Simulation 1.2). The simulations cover vesicles of R = 50–300 nm, near the EV size (40–160 nm in diameter), whereas all experiments used micrometer-sized GUVs, whose membrane is almost flat at the scale of a DNA Bundle. A GUV assay can check the assay itself but cannot test the model; rupture on EV-sized vesicles has not been tested.

04

Lack of Target-Specific Activation

The seed has no activation control (Step 1 is beyond the scope of this project), and cholesterol inserts into any membrane. In standard buffer with 12 mM MgCl2, even unmodified Bundles bound GUVs (Experiment 2.3); 200 mM NaCl suppressed this, but binding depends on buffer as well as cholesterol. Without target-specific regulation, Bundles could attach to non-target vesicles.

3. Future

3.1 Improved Future Design

Improvement 01 addresses Limitations 01–03, and Improvement 02 addresses Limitation 04.

01

Integrated DNA Bundle and Rupture Tests on EV-Sized Vesicles

Approach: Quantify seed dependence, combine elongation and anchoring in one Bundled HCR-grown DNA Bundle, and test rupture on EV-sized vesicles.

  1. Compare gel and AFM with and without the seed, and quantify products and leakage over time; redesign the hairpins if leakage is significant.
  2. Extend the design from 6 to 12 helices (Simulation 1.2) and add cholesterol to the Double-Hairpin DNAs.
  3. Use a short linker (l0 ≈ 2.5 nm) for small vesicles (R ≈ 50 nm) (Simulation 1.1, Figures 2 and 4), and test rupture on nanopore-extruded vesicles by calcein leakage.
02

Selective Seed Activation via Blocking Strands and Target Recognition

Approach: Combine recognition of target EV surface proteins with toehold-mediated strand displacement so that the seed turns on only near the target EV.

Mechanism: A blocking strand pre-hybridized to the seed keeps it OFF [1]. The Trigger DNA, attached to an antibody or part of a DNA aptamer that binds an EV surface protein such as a tetraspanin [2, 3], displaces the blocking strand through a short toehold. In bulk solution the Trigger concentration is too low for this to proceed; on a target EV, bound Triggers lie close to the seeds, raising the local concentration and switching the seed ON only there [4].

3.2 Applications

Selective Physical Disruption of Cancer Cells

Chemotherapies and molecularly targeted drugs often face acquired resistance through drug-efflux pumps, target mutations, and altered signaling [5], so physical approaches that directly disrupt cancer cell membranes are being explored [6, 7]. By attaching the Trigger DNA to antibodies or DNA aptamers that recognize cancer-specific surface antigens [8], our blocked Bundled HCR seed could be activated only on contact with cancer cells, and the resulting rigid DNA Bundles would rupture the plasma membrane independently of intracellular chemical pathways.

Stimuli-Responsive Drug Delivery Systems (DDS)

Conventional liposomal carriers leak drug in the bloodstream and offer poor control over release at disease sites [9]. We propose anchoring blocked Bundled HCR seeds on drug-loaded liposomes: on encountering disease-specific biomarkers, the Trigger DNA would start in situ Bundle assembly, which would rupture the membrane and release the drug locally. For in vivo use, seeds and Triggers would need protection against nucleases, e.g., backbone modification or PEG shielding [10].
