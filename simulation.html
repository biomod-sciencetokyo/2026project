Overview

This page is organized into three linked components:

  1. 2D CG-MD Simulation (Section 1.1)
    A 2D coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulation that screens vesicle radius, DNA Bundle bending rigidity, and linker length for model-defined local pore formation.
  2. 3D Reduced Free-Energy Model (Section 1.2)
    Visualizes three-dimensional membrane deformation and estimates the DNA Bundle helix count required for the design.
  3. Bundled HCR Sequence Design and Thermodynamic Assessment (Section 2)
    Designs the Bundled HCR sequences and evaluates the thermodynamic plausibility of the intended sequential hybridization steps.

Mathematical details, parameters, and source-code information are available in the Technical Appendix.

1. Membrane Deformation Models

1.1 2D CG-MD Simulation

KEY RESULT

Rigid DNA Bundles and size-dependent linker preference

  • Higher DNA Bundle bending rigidity favored membrane pore formation.
  • For small vesicles (\(R = 50\) nm), short linkers were favored.
  • For larger vesicles (\(R = 200\) nm), long linkers were favored.

The 2D model (Figure 1) is used as a computational screening system to identify practical design regions for three programmable parameters: vesicle radius (R), DNA Bundle bending rigidity (BDNA), and linker rest length (l0). Our goal is to estimate which parameter combinations are more likely to produce the model-defined local pore-formation event and thereby guide the design of DNA Bundles for membrane loading.

Each trajectory is evolved as an overdamped Brownian coarse-grained calculation while the DNA Bundle approaches the membrane, using a Brownian-dynamics framework [4]. The readout is the occurrence of a model-defined local pore-formation event: a local spring-extension threshold in the discrete membrane model. This event is used only as a pore-initiation proxy; the 2D model does not resolve physical pore growth, resealing, leakage, or vesicle lysis. Physical pore studies describe additional mechanisms beyond this model-defined criterion [16–22]. Detailed equations, parameters, and procedure are in the Appendix.

2D Comparison: Linker Length, DNA Bundle Bending Rigidity, and Vesicle Size

Linker rest length l0 changes both linker mechanics and the membrane-capture range (26–44 nm across the documented sweep); its influence is not limited to flexibility or distance.

Why the preferred linker length changes

For small vesicles, a short linker is expected to transmit the mechanical loading of a rigid DNA Bundle more directly to a localized membrane region. A longer linker can provide additional relative compliance and attenuate this localized loading. In contrast, for large vesicles, a longer linker increases the model-defined cholesterol–membrane capture range and can allow cholesterol–membrane interaction over a broader accessible region. These are model-level interpretations of the observed trends, not direct evidence of complete membrane rupture or lysis.

Representative trajectories (Figure 2, Figure 3) visualize the contrasting membrane-loading behaviors underlying these design trends.

The heatmaps (Figures 4–9) quantify the fraction of simulated trajectories that reached the model-defined local pore-formation event for each combination of linker rest length and DNA Bundle bending rigidity.

Note on implementation: All simulation results reported on this page (Figures 2–9) were obtained with the original Python implementation (source code). The interactive tool above is a separate JavaScript re-implementation, prepared so that the model can be explored directly in the browser. We have tried to keep the force field, parameters, and integration scheme identical, and in our checks with thermal noise disabled it reproduced the Python output to within numerical precision. Nevertheless, the browser uses a different random-number generator, so individual trajectories will not match the published Python runs, and we cannot completely rule out small implementation differences. Each run lasts 100 frames (100 integration steps of Δt = 10−4 per frame), the same window used for the heatmaps. We therefore recommend treating this tool as an illustrative aid for building intuition about the trends discussed above rather than as a substitute for the Python results; running several seeds may give a rough sense of how the event probability changes across conditions.

1.2 3D Reduced Free-Energy Model

KEY RESULT

12 helices is the robust DNA Bundle design target

Figure 13 gives an ideal fully cross-linked reference of approximately 4 helices. Figure 14 shows that lower crosslinking efficiency (\(\alpha\)) requires more helices; with 12 helices, the target is reached even in the lowest crosslinking-efficiency condition examined. The 13-Helix DNA Bundle is used as a one-helix-above-threshold comparison design.

Why a reduced 3D model?

The 2D CG-MD model compares how DNA Bundle and membrane conditions affect local interactions, but it does not show how the membrane wraps around a DNA Bundle or how deformation spreads in three dimensions. Evaluating every condition with time-evolving 3D CG-MD would be computationally demanding for this project, so we use a 3D Reduced Free-Energy Model instead.

What the model calculates

The 3D model (Figure 10) represents the DNA Bundle lying on a vesicle membrane. For the R0 = 50 nm reference condition, the model estimates a candidate deformation shape from prescribed cholesterol-mediated adhesion, DNA Bundle bending, membrane bending, and membrane-tension terms [1–3, 5–11]. Cholesterol-modified DNA structures have been used for membrane binding and remodeling [12–15].

\[ F_{\mathrm{total}} = E_{\mathrm{adhesion}} + E_{\mathrm{DNA,bend}} + E_{\mathrm{membrane,bend}} + E_{\mathrm{membrane,tension}} \]

\(E_{\mathrm{adhesion}}\) represents cholesterol-mediated effective adhesion.

\(E_{\mathrm{DNA,bend}}\) is the energetic cost of bending the DNA Bundle.

\(E_{\mathrm{membrane,bend}}\) is the local membrane-curvature cost.

\(E_{\mathrm{membrane,tension}}\) is the cost of deforming a tension-bearing membrane.

The full mathematical formulation, numerical update rules, and model-specific assumptions are described in the 3D model details in the Technical Appendix.

Interactive 3D Deformation Visualization

The GLB and deformation views (Figure 11) show local membrane deformation at the DNA Bundle contact region. Its shape reflects the competition between effective adhesion, DNA Bundle flexibility, membrane bending elasticity, and membrane tension. The GLB can be rotated for viewing but is static, not a time-evolving simulation. Its color shows visualization-only local curvature, not a pore-formation test or rupture threshold.

DNA Bundle Radius as a Geometric Design Parameter

DNA Bundle radius is an important geometric factor in membrane deformation. A wider DNA Bundle changes the contact geometry and distributes membrane deformation across a broader region. In contrast, the smaller-radius DNA Bundle (Figure 12) produces more localized membrane deformation and appears more favorable for membrane pore formation. Because the number of helices in a DNA Bundle affects both its radius and bending rigidity, DNA Bundle geometry and mechanical stiffness must be considered together when designing a membrane-active DNA Bundle.

Estimating the Minimum Number of DNA Helices

How to Read Figures 13 and 14

The smaller-radius DNA Bundle in Figure 12 produces more localized membrane deformation, but a DNA Bundle with fewer helices also has lower bending rigidity. We therefore estimated the helix count required to retain sufficient mechanical strength while maintaining a membrane-active DNA Bundle geometry.

Figure 13 shows the ideal fully cross-linked reference condition, in which all DNA helices behave as one rigid DNA Bundle. Under this condition, the target local-curvature level is reached at approximately 4 helices.

In practice, helices may not be perfectly cross-linked. Therefore, Figure 14 evaluates crosslinking efficiency, \(\alpha\), within one DNA Bundle. A larger \(\alpha\) indicates more efficient crosslinking, so the helices behave more like one rigid DNA Bundle; a smaller \(\alpha\) represents less efficient crosslinking and a more flexible DNA Bundle.

As crosslinking efficiency decreases, more helices are required to reach the selected comparison level: approximately 4 helices for \(\alpha=1.0\), 6 for \(\alpha=0.5\), and 12 for \(\alpha=0.1\). We therefore selected the 13-Helix DNA Bundle as a one-helix-above-threshold design.

2. Bundled HCR Sequence Design and Thermodynamic Assessment

To achieve programmable and stimuli-responsive DNA Bundle assembly, we computationally designed and thermodynamically evaluated the DNA sequences of the Bundled HCR system for the 6-Helix DNA Bundle (6HB).

2.1 Bundled HCR Sequence Design

The DNA sequences for the 6HB Bundled HCR system (seed strands S1–S6 and hairpins H1–H6) were designed computationally with NUPACK to eliminate unintended leak reactions and structural defects [23, 24]. Strict physical constraints were applied during the design process:

  1. Complete elimination of ≥4-nt palindromes and homopolymers.
  2. Enforcement of sequence diversity, requiring all four nucleobases (A, T, G, C) to be present within any 7-nt window to prevent local GC-bias.
  3. Multi-tube design analysis (max_size=2) to rigorously suppress homodimerization and nonspecific crosstalk between domains.

2.2 Thermodynamic Assessment of Sequential Hybridization

NUPACK was used to evaluate the equilibrium thermodynamic plausibility of the specified sequential hybridization steps in the designed 6HB Bundled HCR system [24]. First, each hairpin (H1–H6) was analyzed in isolation at 1 µM; the predicted monomer fractions (>99.7%) and homodimer fractions (≤0.21%) indicate that the intended closed-hairpin states are thermodynamically favored under the model conditions. We then calculated the Gibbs free-energy change (ΔG) for each prescribed trigger- or product-associated hybridization step. A negative ΔG means that the intended product complex, including hybridization associated with hairpin opening, is thermodynamically more favorable than the specified reactant state. As summarized in Table 1, all analyzed steps have ΔG < 0. These results support the thermodynamic feasibility of the individual designed steps; they do not predict the reaction rate, opening pathway, full-cascade yield, or irreversible completion of Bundled HCR.
