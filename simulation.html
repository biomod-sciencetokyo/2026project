1. Membrane Disruption

1.1 MD-CG Simulation in 2D Environment

To understand how DNA bundles deform and rupture extracellular vesicle (EV) membranes, we established a two-dimensional coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulation. This cross-sectional model lets us examine the competition between DNA-bundle bending stiffness and lipid-membrane elasticity. The model represents groups of atoms as single beads and includes the DNA bundle, cholesterol anchors, flexible linkers, and the EV membrane lipid bilayer, enabling large-scale structural dynamics to be observed over extended timescales.

2. Kinetics and Thermodynamic Framework

The dynamics of this system are governed by several key biophysical models. To implicitly account for the aqueous solvent environment without rendering every water molecule, the motion of each bead is calculated using Langevin dynamics:

mi dvi/dt = −γivi + FiC + FiR

Here, mi and vi represent the mass and velocity of particle i, and γi is the friction coefficient of the solvent. The stochastic Gaussian noise term (FiR) accurately replicates thermal fluctuations (Brownian motion).

The behavior of the EV membrane is based on Helfrich's continuum elastic theory (Helfrich, 1973), approximated by tuning the angular and linear potentials between the lipid beads to reflect the membrane's bending modulus and area stretch modulus. Furthermore, membrane rupture (lysis) is rigorously determined by a local area strain threshold. When the local strain between lipid beads exceeds a critical limit, the bonds irreversibly break, accurately mimicking the tension-induced bursting of actual lipid bilayers (Evans et al., 2003).

The interactions between cholesterol anchors and the lipid membrane are modeled using a Morse potential, which accurately represents the strong, short-range hydrophobic insertion energies (Bacle et al., 2017). To simulate realistic sequential adhesion, we introduced an entropic zipping mechanism representing the probability of thermally fluctuating linkers stochastically colliding with and binding to the membrane. Finally, the linkers (modeling TEG or poly-T sequences) act as flexible entropic springs that provide mechanical "play" but cannot extend beyond their contour length, instantly transmitting strong tension to the membrane once fully stretched (Kremer & Grest, 1990).

3. Bead-Spring Modeling and DNA Bundle Stiffness

To accurately simulate the mechanical behavior of the DNA bundles, we adopted a bead-spring polymer model where each bundle is discretized into multiple segments. The internal mechanics of this segmented bundle are governed by a bending stiffness that resists changes in angle across consecutive beads.

The critical parameter determining this bending stiffness is based on established mechanical models of DNA nanostructures (Liedl et al., 2010). It is determined by the number of bundled DNA double helices (N) and their crosslinking efficiency (α, ranging from 0 to 1). We derived the effective persistence length Lp,eff by interpolating the uncrosslinked limit (N) and the fully crosslinked rigid-body limit (N2):

Lp,eff = Lp,0 [N(1 − α) + N2α]

where Lp,0 is the base persistence length of a single DNA duplex containing nicks. This macroscopic effective stiffness is then translated into the angular spring constants between the DNA segments in our simulation.

4. 2D Simulation Results: The Crucial Role of Rigidity

As demonstrated in the simulation results, the bending stiffness of the DNA bundle decisively dictates the fate of the EV membrane. The results establish a clear physical principle: the more rigid the DNA bundle, the higher the rupture efficiency.

We compared two distinct scenarios. Under conditions where crosslinking efficiency is low (α ≈ 0.1), the DNA bundle remains flexible. As the vesicle undergoes elliptical deformation, the soft DNA yields to the membrane's inherent curvature, bending its segments to hug the membrane surface via the entropic zipping effect. While this maximizes the number of bound cholesterol anchors, the pulling force becomes broadly dispersed across the entire membrane. Furthermore, the DNA itself absorbs the deformation energy by bending, buffering the applied tension. Consequently, the local strain fails to accumulate sufficiently, and the membrane deformation fails (the vesicle remains intact).

In stark contrast, under conditions with high crosslinking efficiency (α ≈ 0.8), the DNA bundle forms a highly rigid structure. This rigid scaffold acts like a lever, strongly resisting any bending along the deforming membrane. As a result, the physical pulling forces are locally concentrated at the few contact points near the edges. Because the rigid DNA does not absorb tension through its own deformation, the physical stress is 100% transmitted through the fully stretched linkers to the lipid bilayer. This focused stress instantly causes the local strain to surpass the critical rupture threshold, successfully tearing the membrane.

5. Simulation Parameters and Physical Justification

The simulation utilizes parameters rigorously grounded in biophysics to ensure the model remains stable and realistic, even when scaled up to Giant Unilamellar Vesicles (GUVs).

Thermodynamic Environment
  • kBT = 4.11 pN·nm: Thermal energy at room temperature (approximately 298 K).
  • γ = 1.0 pN·s/nm: Friction coefficient of the solvent.
  • dt = 0.0001: Time step. To prevent numerical explosion caused by the extreme forces of rigid DNA, a maximum displacement cap per step is applied.
Membrane Mechanics (DOPC-based)
  • kbend_mem = 25 kBT: Membrane bending rigidity (Dimova, 2014).
  • Critical strain = 0.35 (35%): The local rupture threshold. Real membranes rupture at 3–5% global area strain (Evans et al., 2003); in our coarse-grained scaling, 35% local strain provides a physically robust buffer against thermal noise while ensuring tension-induced lysis.
  • Scale-independent area constraint: To stabilize GUV scales (R = 300 nm), internal volume pressure is calculated using relative area strain rather than absolute area difference, preventing numerical divergence.
DNA and Linker Dynamics
  • Nchains = 12: Number of bundled DNA chains.
  • Lp,0 = 25 nm: Base persistence length, adjusted downward from 50 nm to account for nicks and gaps in HCR polymers.
  • De = 60 kBT: Cholesterol insertion energy for the Morse potential, mimicking strong, short-range hydrophobic anchoring (Bacle et al., 2017).
  • Lcontour: Maximum extension length of the TEG/poly-T linkers. They act as ultra-soft entropic springs (klinker = 1.5 pN/nm) until this limit is reached, mimicking realistic polymer "play."

1.2 3D Thermodynamic Simulation

To accurately investigate the conditions required for membrane disruption, we performed a 3D thermodynamic stabilization calculation. The primary reason for adopting this approach is that it allows us to visualize the thermodynamically stable positions of the DNA bundle and the liposome in a static system, enabling detailed observation of the stereoscopic deformation process. Furthermore, by establishing a critical curvature limit, we can effectively determine whether the membrane will rupture, making it a powerful method for exploring the exact conditions of membrane disruption. In our modeling, we established a system that finds the equilibrium state by minimizing the total free energy, which consists of the membrane's bending and tension energies, the DNA's bending energy, and the adhesion energy from the cholesterol anchors.

Details of the 3D Thermodynamic Simulation Equations and Parameter Settings

This simulation models the search for a thermodynamic equilibrium state that minimizes the total free energy of the system, Ftotal. Ftotal is defined as the sum of three energy terms:

Ftotal = Eadhesion + Edna_bend + Emembrane

The calculations are based on the thermal energy at room temperature (300 K), kBT ≈ 4.11 × 10−21 J.

Cholesterol Adhesion Energy (Eadhesion)

The adhesion energy per cholesterol molecule due to hydrophobic insertion, εchol, was set to 4.5 kBT (Baumgart et al., Nature, 2003). For a DNA bundle of length Lbundle = 60 nm modified with Nchol = 22 cholesterols, the total theoretical adhesion energy is:

Eadh,total = 22 × 4.5 kBT = 99 kBT

The simulation calculates the actual energy gained proportionally to the contact area, which depends on the membrane indentation depth (zindent) and the bundle radius (rdna).

DNA Bundle Bending Energy (Edna_bend)

The DNA bundle is treated as an elastic beam based on Euler-Bernoulli beam theory. The energy cost for a bundle with rigidity Bdna to bend at a radius of curvature Rbend is:

Edna_bend = ½ Bdna (1/Rbend)2 Lbundle

Membrane Deformation Energy (Emembrane)

Based on Helfrich theory, we calculate the deformation cost of the target liposome with an initial radius R0 = 50 nm. We used a membrane bending rigidity κm = 20 kBT (Rawicz et al., Biophysical Journal, 2000) and a membrane fluctuation tension γm = 1.0 × 10−4 mN/m. The bending and tension penalties are calculated using the effective deformation depth (zeff), which accounts for the geometric mismatch between the straight DNA rod and the spherical liposome, and the width of the membrane deformation pocket (wy).

Using SciPy's L-BFGS-B optimization algorithm, we simultaneously optimize these three variables— zindent, Rbend, and wy— to find the geometric solution where Ftotal is minimized.

Modeling Objectives & Structural Scale

The model finds the thermodynamically stable configuration by minimizing the total free energy contributed by membrane bending and tension, DNA bending, and cholesterol-anchor adhesion. Establishing a critical average curvature of 0.067 nm-1 provides a threshold for determining whether the membrane is predicted to rupture.

By using the L-BFGS-B optimization algorithm to solve for the equilibrium state where the membrane's elastic energy (based on Helfrich theory) competes with the DNA's bending energy (based on Euler-Bernoulli beam theory), we constructed 3D curvature heatmaps. This spatially proved that when a highly rigid, straight DNA bundle adheres to a liposome, the membrane is sharply folded at the bundle's edges, causing extreme stress concentration.

Subsequent parameter exploration revealed that not only the "bending rigidity" but also the "bundle radius (thickness)" is crucial for membrane rupture. While increased rigidity severely distorts the membrane, a thicker bundle spreads the stress laterally due to a wider contact area, thereby reducing local curvature. Since both of these physical scalings depend directly on the number of bundled DNA strands (N), we reasoned that we could theoretically calculate the minimum number of strands required to disrupt the membrane.

Minimum Bundle Requirements & Cross-linking Efficiency

We performed a 2D scaling analysis, plotting the number of strands (N) on the X-axis against the maximum membrane curvature (Hmax) on the Y-axis. Assuming an ideal rigid DNA bundle (cross-linking efficiency α = 1.0), the bundle's radius is calculated as r ≈ 1.25√N, and its bending rigidity increases proportionally to the square of the number of strands, B ∝ α · N2. When these parameters are fed back into our 3D stabilization simulation, the curvature initially spikes due to the explosive increase in rigidity, but eventually, the stress dispersion from the increasing radius takes over, creating a "convex saturation curve." In this calculation, the curve breached the critical membrane rupture threshold (0.067 nm−1) at approximately 6 DNA helices. This quantitatively demonstrates that under ideal, perfectly cross-linked conditions, a minimum of 6 DNA strands is required to initiate membrane disruption.

2. Design and Thermodynamic Validation of 6-Helix Bundle HCR Sequences

To achieve programmable and stimuli-responsive bundle assembly, we computationally designed and thermodynamically evaluated the DNA sequences for the 6-Helix Bundle HCR system.

2.1 Sequence Design Conditions

The DNA sequences for the 6-Helix Bundle HCR system (seeds S1-S6 and hairpins H1-H6) were computationally optimized using NUPACK to eliminate unintended leak reactions and structural defects. Strict physical constraints were applied during the design process:

  1. Complete elimination of ≥4-nt palindromes and homopolymers.
  2. Enforcement of sequence diversity, requiring all four nucleobases (A, T, G, C) to be present within any 7-nt window to prevent local GC-bias.
  3. Multi-tube optimization (max_size=2) to rigorously suppress homodimerization and non-specific crosstalk between domains.

2.2 Thermodynamic Feasibility of the HCR Cascade

Thermodynamic simulations at 25°C confirmed the high structural integrity and reactivity of the designed system. In isolation, the hairpin strands strongly favor the target double-hairpin monomer state, with dimerization rates suppressed to near zero (<0.1%). Furthermore, the hybridization chain reaction is thermodynamically highly favorable; every sequential binding step in the cascade yields a substantial negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG ≪ 0). This steep “energy cascade” ensures that the self-assembly into the final 6-Helix Bundle proceeds spontaneously, rapidly, and irreversibly upon trigger initiation.
