Overview

This page presents two distinct mechanical models: a finite-time 2D coarse-grained simulation that screens for a model-defined local pore-formation event, and a 3D constrained reduced free-energy optimization that generates a static deformation visualization. The 3D visualization is not a time-evolving simulation; neither model establishes complete membrane rupture or vesicle lysis. We also assess the thermodynamic feasibility of the HCR design for building DNA Bundles.

The 2D model is used as a computational screening system. Its GOAL is to examine how the THREE PROGRAMMABLE PARAMETERS—vesicle radius (R), DNA Bundle bending rigidity (BDNA), and linker rest length (l0)—shape DNA Bundle–membrane interactions. Because changing R also rescales the membrane discretization, Bundle length, anchor number, and other geometry-dependent quantities, radius-dependent trends should be interpreted as the response of the jointly scaled model system rather than as curvature alone.

Each trajectory is evolved as a finite-time overdamped Brownian coarse-grained calculation while the DNA Bundle approaches the membrane. The readout is the occurrence of a model-defined local pore-formation event: a local spring-extension threshold in the discrete membrane model. This event is used only as a pore-initiation proxy; the 2D model does not resolve physical pore growth, resealing, leakage, or vesicle lysis. Detailed equations, parameters, and procedure are in the Appendix.

Unlike the representative GIFs, each heatmap summarizes the model-defined local pore-formation-event probability across simulated trajectories for combinations of linker length and Bundle bending rigidity. Each map shows a different vesicle radius.

Across the current parameter space, high DNA Bundle bending rigidity was a common feature of conditions with elevated model-defined local pore-formation-event probabilities. In the heat maps obtained for the smaller-vesicle conditions, high-probability regions were predominantly observed for a DNA Bundle with high bending rigidity combined with a short linker. In contrast, in the larger-vesicle conditions, the high-probability region shifted toward combinations of a DNA Bundle with high bending rigidity and a longer linker.

We therefore describe the result as a radius-dependent crossover within the present model. Because changing R also changes Bundle length, anchor count, repulsion discretization, and friction, this is not an isolated curvature effect.

1.2 3D Reduced Free-Energy Model

Why a reduced 3D model?

The 2D CG-MD model compares how DNA Bundle and membrane conditions affect local interactions, but it does not show how the membrane wraps around a Bundle or how deformation spreads in three dimensions. Evaluating every condition with time-evolving 3D CG-MD would be computationally demanding for this project, so we use a 3D Reduced Free-Energy Model instead.

What the model calculates

For the R0 = 50 nm reference condition, the model finds a static candidate shape that minimizes its reduced free energy, balancing cholesterol-mediated effective adhesion, DNA Bundle bending, membrane bending, and membrane tension. It is a constrained reduced model, not a time-evolving 3D simulation.

Interactive 3D Deformation Visualization

The GLB and deformation views show local membrane deformation at the DNA Bundle contact region. Its shape reflects the competition between effective adhesion, Bundle flexibility, membrane bending elasticity, and membrane tension. The GLB can be rotated for viewing but is static, not a time-evolving simulation. Its color shows visualization-only local curvature, not a pore-formation test or rupture threshold.

Bundle Radius as a Geometric Design Parameter

DNA Bundle geometry is an important factor in three-dimensional membrane deformation. The Bundle radius determines the width of the membrane-contact region and the geometric extent over which the membrane must bend around the Bundle. In contrast, effective bending rigidity determines how strongly the Bundle itself resists bending. These two Bundle properties therefore affect deformation through different mechanisms.

Low bundle radius (r = 4 nm).

High bundle radius (r = 10 nm).

A wider DNA Bundle changes the contact geometry and distributes membrane deformation across a broader region. This radius comparison is a qualitative geometric illustration, not a quantitative radius-sweep result or a prediction of pore formation. Bundle radius is a geometrically important input parameter to include in future parameter sweeps.

The radius comparison suggests an important DNA Bundle design trade-off. A smaller Bundle radius concentrates membrane deformation within a narrower contact region and is therefore expected to favor the localized membrane deformation associated with the local pore-formation proxy used in this study. However, a DNA Bundle composed of fewer helices is also expected to have a lower effective bending rigidity.

Our 2D screening model indicated that, under comparable conditions, a mechanically stiffer DNA Bundle more readily produces the model-defined local pore-formation event. Therefore, the optimal design is not simply the smallest possible Bundle. Instead, the Bundle should remain sufficiently narrow to localize membrane deformation while containing enough DNA helices to retain the mechanical rigidity required for effective loading.

We therefore performed a helix-number screening analysis to estimate the minimum number of DNA helices required to reach a selected local-curvature comparison level under each α condition. Here, N denotes the number of DNA helices within one DNA Bundle, not the number of independently attacking DNA Bundles around a vesicle.

This threshold is a rigidity-based design estimate. The present analysis does not yet quantitatively combine the radius-dependent geometric effect with the helix-number-dependent stiffness effect; instead, it identifies a practical lower bound on Bundle size needed to maintain sufficient effective rigidity.

To explore how incomplete coupling between helices may alter Bundle mechanics, we introduced α as a dimensionless, model-defined cross-linking-efficiency parameter. In this screening model, the effective Bundle-rigidity factor is defined as:

Beff = α C N2 Lbundle

Here, N is the number of DNA helices, Lbundle is the Bundle length, and C is a fixed model scaling constant. A larger α represents a more mechanically integrated DNA Bundle and therefore a larger effective bending-rigidity factor.

The predicted local-curvature indicator is then calculated from the mechanical balance between the Bundle and membrane:

κBundle = [kmem / (Beff + kmem)] × (1 / R0)

Hmax = (1 / R0) + 3 [(1 / R0) − κBundle]

This is a phenomenological screening model rather than a calibrated material law. We used α = 1.0, 0.5, and 0.1 as high-, intermediate-, and low-effective cross-linking conditions. These values were selected to test sensitivity over a broad range of assumed mechanical coupling; they are not experimentally measured cross-linking efficiencies.

2. Design and Thermodynamic Validation of 6-Helix Bundle HCR Sequences

To achieve programmable and stimuli-responsive bundle assembly, we computationally designed and thermodynamically evaluated the DNA sequences for the 6-Helix Bundle HCR system.

2.1 Sequence Design Conditions

The DNA sequences for the 6-Helix Bundle HCR system (seeds S1-S6 and hairpins H1-H6) were computationally optimized using NUPACK to eliminate unintended leak reactions and structural defects. Strict physical constraints were applied during the design process:

  1. Complete elimination of ≥4-nt palindromes and homopolymers.
  2. Enforcement of sequence diversity, requiring all four nucleobases (A, T, G, C) to be present within any 7-nt window to prevent local GC-bias.
  3. Multi-tube optimization (max_size=2) to rigorously suppress homodimerization and non-specific crosstalk between domains.

2.2 Thermodynamic Feasibility of the HCR Cascade

To verify the structural integrity and reaction feasibility of the designed 6-Helix Bundle HCR system, thermodynamic simulations were conducted using NUPACK. The monomer fraction of each hairpin (H1–H6) in isolation (1 μM) was evaluated, demonstrating exceptional structural fidelity (>99.7% monomeric purity) with negligible homodimerization (≤0.21%). The thermodynamic driving force for the HCR cascade was assessed by calculating the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for each sequential binding event initialized by the trigger strand (S2). As summarized in Table 1, every hybridization step exhibits a substantial negative free energy change (ΔG < 0), establishing a robust energetic cascade that guarantees the spontaneous and irreversible assembly of the 6-Helix Bundle.
